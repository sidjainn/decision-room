# Ansel Judged Demo: One Team, Three Fires

## Demo promise

In five minutes, three judges become leaders competing for one product team's next sprint. The presenter plays their Chief of Staff. Ansel listens to the live disagreement, separates shared ground from the real trade-off, and helps the group leave with a decision they can execute.

The point is not to make Ansel decide. The point is to show that Ansel makes a messy leadership conversation legible quickly enough for the people in the room to decide.

## The scenario

It is Monday morning at a 60-person B2B software company. The only available product squad has **10 effort points** for the next two weeks. Three urgent requests have arrived:

- A large prospect wants a reporting feature before Friday.
- Existing customers saw two reliability incidents last month.
- Trial users are abandoning the onboarding flow.

All three requests matter. They cannot all be fully delivered this sprint. The room must agree on what the team will actually do, what it will deliberately defer, and what promise can be made outside the room.

## Room setup

Prepare this 5–10 minutes before presenting:

1. Put the Ansel room on one laptop connected to the main display.
2. Place the listening device centrally, with its microphone unobstructed. Any joined participant can start Ansel from their device; use only one listening device at a time during the demo.
3. Create the room using the exact text in **Copy-paste room data** below.
4. Open **Invite** and leave the QR code ready.
5. Give each judge one private role card. Ask them not to show the card to the other judges.
6. If time permits, have judges scan the QR and join using the name and role on their card. If not, begin immediately; joining is visual polish, not required for the conversation.
7. Keep the three final decision options visible only to the presenter until the closing beat.

Use three judges for the cleanest run. If there are only two, combine Product and Engineering. If there are four, give the fourth judge the optional CEO role below.

## Cast and private role cards

The presenter is **Maya, Chief of Staff**. Maya has no preferred project. Her job is to get a clear, defensible decision and name what is being deferred.

Cut or message the following sections separately so each judge sees only their own card.

### Judge 1 — Alex, VP Sales

**Say early:** “The Northstar deal is worth ₹1.2 crore annually. If they cannot see custom reporting by Friday, they may walk. I want six points on the reporting feature.”

**Your private motivation:** You have already told the CEO the deal is likely to close this quarter. Losing it will hurt your forecast and credibility.

**What you know but should reveal only if asked:** The buyer needs a convincing working demo and a dated delivery commitment; they do not require production rollout this Friday. A three-point prototype could protect the deal.

**Your acceptable compromise:** A three-point prototype this sprint, a customer demo on Friday, and a written date for production delivery.

**Push once:** “A generic promise will not protect the deal. Who owns the Friday demo and the follow-up date?”

### Judge 2 — Priya, VP Engineering

**Say early:** “We had two incidents in 30 days. Shipping another feature before stabilising the system is irresponsible. I want all ten points on reliability.”

**Your private motivation:** Your team is tired, and another incident could trigger an enterprise security review. You do not want revenue pressure to normalise risky work.

**What you know but should reveal only if asked:** Five points would fix the two known failure modes and add the most important alerts. The other five points are valuable hardening, but not required to reduce the immediate risk.

**Your acceptable compromise:** Five protected reliability points, no production shortcut for the sales feature, and the prototype isolated from customer data.

**Push once:** “I can support a demo prototype, but not if it quietly becomes production scope.”

### Judge 3 — Jordan, VP Product

**Say early:** “Forty percent of trial users never finish setup. If we keep pausing onboarding, we keep pouring leads into a leaking bucket. I want five points on onboarding and five on reliability.”

**Your private motivation:** Activation is your quarterly OKR, and this is the third sprint in which Sales has asked to interrupt the roadmap.

**What you know but should reveal only if asked:** A two-point instrumentation and design spike would identify the biggest drop-off and keep next sprint's fix credible. You do not need the full onboarding rebuild now.

**Your acceptable compromise:** A two-point spike now, with an explicit checkpoint that gives the onboarding fix first claim on the next sprint if the data confirms the issue.

**Push once:** “I will not accept ‘later.’ What evidence and date bring onboarding back into the decision?”

### Optional Judge 4 — Sam, CEO

**Say only after the first round:** “I care about all three outcomes, but I will not approve twelve points disguised as ten. I need one accountable plan by the end of this meeting.”

**Your private motivation:** You want to see whether the team can trade scope instead of defending functions.

**Your acceptable compromise:** Any plan that fits ten points, protects customer trust, and names owners plus dates.

Do not introduce a new initiative. If the group reaches a clean plan, ask: “What exactly are we telling the work that did not make the cut?”

## Five-minute run of show

### 0:00–0:30 — Hook and roles

Presenter:

> “You are my leadership team for the next five minutes. We have one product squad, ten effort points, and three legitimate fires. Please play the position on your card. You can negotiate, but do not give away your private fallback unless someone uncovers it.”

If judges have phones ready, let them scan **Invite** and join. Then select **Start Ansel**.

Presenter:

> “Ansel, you are joining our sprint decision. Listen for what we agree on and where the real trade-off sits.”

### 0:30–1:35 — Deliberate collision

Presenter:

> “Alex, Priya, Jordan: give me your recommendation and the consequence of not choosing it. Twenty seconds each.”

Let each judge deliver the **Say early** line in turn. Do not solve or summarise for them. Allow one brief challenge between judges.

**Expected agreement to emerge:** Revenue, customer trust, and activation all matter; the plan must fit ten points; doing nothing is not acceptable.

**Expected disagreement to emerge:** The group appears to be choosing between a six-point sales build, a ten-point reliability sprint, and a five-point onboarding investment. The hidden issue is that each request has been framed as a full solution rather than its smallest risk-reducing slice.

### 1:35–2:15 — Ask Ansel to find the hinge

Presenter:

> “Ansel, what do you hear us agreeing on, and what is the one disagreement we need to resolve first?”

Let Ansel answer. Then glance at the **Aligned** and **Still different** columns as they update.

A useful intervention should sound roughly like this:

> “You agree the sprint must protect customer trust and near-term growth without exceeding ten points. The unresolved question is not which goal matters; it is the minimum credible scope each goal requires. What is the smallest commitment that changes the risk for each of you?”

The wording will vary. That is desirable: the demo should feel like a live conversation, not a playback.

### 2:15–3:10 — Reveal the negotiable minimums

Presenter, to each leader:

> “What is the smallest safe or credible version of your request, and what condition must come with it?”

The judges now reveal their acceptable compromises:

- Sales: 3-point isolated prototype + Friday demo + production date.
- Engineering: 5 protected reliability points + no production shortcut.
- Product: 2-point onboarding measurement/design spike + dated next-sprint checkpoint.

Presenter:

> “That is ten points: five reliability, three sales prototype, two onboarding spike. Before we call it, who sees a hidden failure in that package?”

Allow one final push from any role.

### 3:10–4:10 — Stress-test the proposal

Presenter:

> “Ansel, stress-test this ten-point package. What commitment is still too vague for us to execute?”

Use Ansel's question or observation to tighten one detail. If Ansel identifies a different ambiguity, follow it. Otherwise prompt the owners directly:

- Engineering owns the five-point reliability work and confirms prototype isolation today.
- Sales owns Friday's prototype demo and sends the prospect a production date after engineering review.
- Product owns the two-point onboarding spike and brings findings to next sprint planning.
- Maya records a 15-minute checkpoint for the following Thursday.

### 4:10–5:00 — Close visibly

Presenter:

> “Thumbs up if you can support this plan and execute your part; sideways if you can live with it but want a risk recorded; down if it is unsafe.”

If there is no thumb down, close with:

> “Decision: five points to reliability, three to an isolated Northstar prototype, and two to an onboarding spike. The full reporting release and onboarding rebuild are explicitly deferred. Owners and dates are named. Ansel, give us the decision and the one risk we must carry forward in one sentence.”

End by pointing to how the live map distinguishes common ground from the remaining tension. Do not claim unanimous enthusiasm; “supportable and executable” is the win.

## Final decision options

Use these only if the live conversation needs a concrete choice. All options fit the ten-point cap.

### Option A — Balanced risk reduction (target ending)

- 5 points: fix the two known reliability failure modes and add critical alerts.
- 3 points: build an isolated Northstar reporting prototype for Friday's demo.
- 2 points: instrument onboarding drop-off and design the highest-impact fix.
- Deferred: production reporting release and full onboarding rebuild.

This is the strongest demo ending because every leader trades full scope for a credible minimum, and Ansel can make that shared logic visible.

### Option B — Trust first

- 8 points: reliability fixes and hardening.
- 2 points: onboarding instrumentation.
- Deferred: Northstar prototype and all production reporting work.

Choose this if judges surface evidence that the prototype cannot be safely isolated or another incident is imminent.

### Option C — Revenue deadline first

- 5 points: reliability fixes for the known failure modes.
- 5 points: a larger Northstar prototype and integration test.
- Deferred: onboarding work.

Choose this if Sales establishes that a three-point prototype will not satisfy the buyer. Record the activation risk and give Product a dated re-entry checkpoint; otherwise this is not a complete decision.

## If judges improvise

Do not pull them back to memorised lines. Use the improvisation to show facilitation.

| What happens | Chief of Staff response | What Ansel should help expose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Everyone agrees immediately | “Before we settle, what could make this plan fail by Friday?” | Hidden conditions, risk, or owner ambiguity |
| Everyone refuses to move | “Name the outcome you protect, then the smallest scope that protects it.” | Positions versus underlying interests |
| A judge invents a fourth request | “What existing work loses points, or is this next-sprint work?” | Capacity must stay at ten |
| A judge disputes a number | “What evidence would change the estimate, and what safe assumption do we use today?” | Fact, assumption, and reversible next step |
| One judge dominates | “I have your position. I need the failure mode the other leaders see.” | Missing perspective without shaming the speaker |
| Judges negotiate a better plan | Accept it if it fits ten points; ask for owners, dates, and explicit deferrals. | Whether the agreement is executable |
| A judge stays in character and blocks | Ask for a thumb-down reason, then move to Option B or C if the risk is real. | A legitimate blocker versus preference |
| Ansel is quiet | Address it directly: “Ansel, summarise our common ground and ask one question that would unlock this.” | A concise, relevant intervention |
| Ansel or the network lags | Continue the human exchange for 20 seconds, then ask once more. If unavailable, use the visible transcript/map already captured and close the decision yourself. | The demo remains about better group reasoning, not a perfect voice turn |

Never pretend the board or Ansel said something it did not. If the live map phrases a point imperfectly, say: “That is close; let me sharpen it,” and state the corrected version aloud.

## Presenter safety checklist

- Start with a fresh room so no old transcript or alignment points appear.
- Confirm microphone permission and that Ansel can hear a plain test sentence.
- Keep judge role cards private; the reveal of minimum acceptable scope creates the demo's turn.
- Ask one person to speak at a time and keep responses to about 20 seconds.
- Watch the live map, but keep eye contact with the judges. It is evidence of the conversation, not the conversation itself.
- Close on a decision, owners, deferred work, and a review date even if the AI response varies.
- Stop Ansel before moving to questions.

## What success looks like to the judges

By the end, the judges should have experienced three things rather than merely heard claims about them:

1. Their initially incompatible positions were captured without flattening the disagreement.
2. Ansel identified a useful hinge: full requests could be reframed as minimum credible commitments.
3. The room left with a capacity-valid plan, explicit deferrals, owners, and a follow-up—not a vague summary.
